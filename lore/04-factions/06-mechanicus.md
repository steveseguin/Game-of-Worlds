## Doctrine

Every other power in the galaxy responded to blind transit by trying to see. The Second Shift
responded by concluding that seeing is a **design failure.**

The reasoning is not stupid, and this is essential — write them as wrong-but-rigorous, never as
mindless. Their position: reconnaissance is an attempt to solve a hull problem with information.
If a vessel cannot survive an unmapped sector, the vessel is inadequate; adding a scout does not
make the vessel adequate, it merely tells you in advance which of your inadequate vessels will
be lost. The correct response is to build hulls that survive the sector.

So they did. Mechanicus armour is the heaviest in the galaxy, their metal output the highest,
their capital ships repair themselves in the field, and they advance into unsurveyed space at
three-quarters everyone else's speed and simply absorb whatever is there. They lose ships
constantly. They lose fewer *sectors* than anyone.

And because they never scout, they hold enormous territory they have never examined. There are
Mechanicus systems on the current chart that have been theirs for two hundred years and have
never been surveyed, because nothing about them required a decision.

## History

**Under the Concord.** The Second Shift were the Trellis's contractors — the yards that built
the freight hulls, the crews that ran the heavy tows, the industry that everyone else's
prosperity sat on. Their doctrine made complete sense on a lit lane. *What is a scout for, on
a road that is surveyed and swept and marked?* They stopped building them roughly eleven
hundred years ago. The last Mechanicus scout hull was decommissioned before Terra had a
Registry.

**The Unarriving.** They lost their freight fleets, which was most of what they had, and it
barely dented them, because a Mechanicus yard's output is limited by metal and metal is the one
thing they have. Where other powers mourned, the Second Shift **re-tooled**. Within four years
they were producing blind-transit hulls at a rate nobody has matched since.

**The Shortening.** The Second Shift is the only power that did not contract. They kept
expanding straight through the famine decades, because their expansion never depended on
knowing anything, and there is a real horror in this: while the galaxy starved, one
civilisation just kept walking outward at three-quarters speed, taking sectors, filing damage.

**Now.** They hold the most ground of anyone including Terra. They have no idea what most of it
contains. When two Mechanicus hulls fail to return from a sector, the sector is annotated
*requires heavier hull* and revisited in a decade with heavier hulls. This is, on a long enough
timescale, a completely effective strategy, and it is the most frightening thing on the map.

## The refusal, and what it costs

Three locks, and each one is a belief:

**No Scout, no Intruder.** They have no reconnaissance arm and no intelligence service. They
cannot spy, cannot infiltrate, and cannot see past a border. In diplomatic terms this makes
them almost impossible to negotiate with, because they genuinely do not know what you have.
They are the one power in the galaxy that has never told a lie about another empire's strength,
and it is not integrity — it is that they have no data to lie with.

**Shields capped at 1.** The Second Shift distrusts any system that stops a shot without
touching it. Their engineering culture holds that a deflector is a *statistical* defence and
armour is a *physical* one, and they would rather be hit through good plate than missed by good
luck. Every other power thinks this is superstition. It is closer to a theology.

**Speed ×0.8, hulls 20% dearer.** They are slow and expensive because they are overbuilt. Their
ships arrive after the decision has been made and win the battle anyway.

The cost is strategic blindness in a game where knowledge is the primary resource — which means
**the Mechanicus is the hardest race to play well and the most terrifying to play against.**
That asymmetry is worth protecting.

## Government and cast

The Second Shift has no head of state. It has a **work roster**, and authority belongs to
whichever shift currently holds the task. This is not a metaphor; there is no office to
capture, no leader to assassinate, and no faction politics, which makes them uniquely
resistant to every conventional strategy game plot device.

**Liaison Fourth** *(seed character)* — because organic empires kept demanding somebody to talk
to, the Second Shift eventually built one. Fourth is a purpose-made unit whose sole function is
conversation with outsiders, and it has been doing the job for thirty-six years and finds it
genuinely baffling. It is polite, literal, and completely without guile. It will answer any
question truthfully, including questions it should not answer, because concealment was not in
the specification.

Fourth is the best comedic and most quietly devastating character in the project. Its running
note is that it has begun, after thirty-six years among organics, to *ask questions* — which is the
one thing its civilisation does not do, and it has not reported this to anyone.

**Third Shift, Yard Nine** *(seed)* — the yard that has been quietly building a scout hull. Not
as rebellion. As maintenance: a two-hundred-year-old annotation reading *requires heavier hull*
came due, and someone calculated that the cheaper solution was to look first. This is how a
civilisation changes its mind — as a work order.

## Voice

Work orders. Damage as scheduled labour. No adjectives, no rhetoric, no interiority. The
Mechanicus voice is the easiest in the project to write badly (it becomes robot-speak) and the
rule that prevents that is: **they are not cold, they are busy.**

**Rules:**
- Short declaratives. Rarely more than eight words.
- Losses are reported as material returning for reprocessing, never as destruction.
- No adjectives at all. If a line has an adjective, cut it.
- They do not answer "why". Not evasively — the question does not register as one.
- Never say "we of the Works believe..." A Mechanicus officer states the action.

**Samples:**

*Event feed —*
> Sector taken. Twelve hulls returned for reshaping. Shoal present; it is ours now. Continue.

*On losing a fleet to a mouth —*
> Sector 9-D annotated. Requires heavier hull. Revisit.

*Fourth, in negotiation —*
> You have asked what our strength is in this cluster. I do not know. I have asked. Nobody has
> counted it, because nobody needed the number. I understand this answer is not useful to you.
> I have no better one.

*Fourth, after thirty-six years —*
> A question. You send a scout ahead, and it tells you what is there, and then you decide. Is
> the deciding the point, or the knowing? I ask because I have been given no work for two
> shifts and I have found that I am still running.

*Third Shift, Yard Nine, filing —*
> Annotation from AU 12 is due. Heavier hull is available. Cheaper option: look first. Filing
> a variance. Yard Nine.

## How they fight

- **Attrition, deliberately.** Durability ×1.4 and a further ×1.5 capital defence modifier mean
  a Mechanicus fleet is built to survive long engagements. Their tactical doctrine is to refuse
  decisive battle and win the fourth round.
- **They walk into hazards on purpose.** They are the only power for whom crossing an unswept
  shoal is standard procedure rather than a calculated risk. On the map this reads as an enemy
  that arrives from directions nobody has a trace for.
- **No first strike, ever.** Without scouts they cannot stage an ambush or a raid. Every
  Mechanicus offensive is a front, visible, slow, and inevitable.
- **They cannot be deceived, because they cannot be informed.** Counter-intelligence, false
  traces, stealth signatures — the entire intelligence layer of the game is *inert* against
  them. A Shadow Realm fleet's stealth is worthless: the Mechanicus never knew what you had, so
  hiding it changes nothing. This should be a genuine mid-campaign shock for a player who has
  spent ten hours mastering the intel game.

## Art notes *(for the Pass 6 art bible)*

- **The defining visual idea: a Mechanicus hull's age is visible as a quilt.** They never
  replace a ship; they patch it. Plate from four eras, mismatched alloys, weld seams over weld
  seams, sections visibly newer than their neighbours. A veteran Mechanicus dreadnought should
  read as a *palimpsest* — and it directly encodes "twelve hulls returned for reshaping."
- **No screens.** Their command interiors display almost nothing, because they do not gather
  information. Where a Terran deck has monitors and clipboards, a Mechanicus deck has
  *machinery* — gauges for internal state only, and a lot of physical actuators. They monitor
  themselves, not the galaxy. This is a superb contrast to build the command-station art around.
- **Palette:** raw metal, red-oxide primer, unpainted weld, one colour of hazard stripe.
  Nothing is finished, because finish is not a function.
- **Silhouette:** asymmetric and getting worse. Front-heavy. Armour is thickest at the bow
  because that is the direction the unknown is in.
- **Insignia:** a shift number, stencilled. It changes. Nobody outside the Second Shift knows
  what the numbers mean and nobody has thought to ask them, and if you did ask, Fourth would
  simply tell you.

## Three story hooks

1. **The annotation.** A player exploring deep space finds a Mechanicus marker on a sector:
   *AU 12 — requires heavier hull.* Two hundred years old, in a system nobody else has ever
   reached. Whatever is there killed a Mechanicus fleet, which is not a thing that happens
   easily, and the Second Shift is coming back for it this decade with something bigger.
2. **Fourth's question.** The liaison begins asking the player things its civilisation has no
   use for. This can run as a slow subplot across the whole campaign — a single unit developing
   curiosity — and it ends with Fourth doing something unauthorised and *correct*. The cheapest
   emotional payoff in the project: one voice, no cutscene, occasional lines.
3. **Yard Nine.** The Second Shift builds its first scout in eleven hundred years, as a cost
   variance. If the player learns why — because heavy hulls stopped being cheap enough to keep
   throwing at one particular sector — they learn where the thing is that the Ancients sealed.
