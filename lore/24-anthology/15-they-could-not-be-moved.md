# 15 · They Could Not Be Moved

One long piece rather than the usual eight short ones. It is the payoff for
`05-buildings.md` — *"Orbital Turret — Eighty Metal, Sixty Crystal, and the Argument"* — and it belongs
to `../28-through-lines.md` T7, because a turret is the only military purchase in this setting that is
not a wager.

Everything terrible in it comes from a law that already exists. Nothing here needed inventing.

---

## The account of Battery Warden Arran, Ordel Gate Approaches, taken AU 74

I voted against them. I want that first, because everything after it is worth less if I put it later.

I was a costing deputy for eleven years and I signed the minority assessment in AU 63 and it is in the
file under my own name. *Fixed orbital emplacements: eighty metal and sixty crystal apiece, no
mobility, no pursuit capability, no demonstrated engagement value.* I wrote *no demonstrated engagement
value* about the thing that is the reason my daughter can read this.

There is a man called Deshan who commanded eleven batteries and told anyone who would sit still that a
turret was a promise. I read his statements. I found them sentimental. I costed a warship at fourteen
batteries and I wrote that a warship can be *sent*, and I was correct about that, and it is the most
expensive true thing I have ever written down.

---

### The argument, which lasted nine years and which we won

You have to understand how reasonable we were.

A battery fires perhaps twice in its service life. Deshan's fired four shots in AU 71 at a raider and
hit nothing, and the raider left, and the Admiralty's assessment of his battery's effectiveness that
year was **nil**. That is not a slander. That is the actual word in the actual column. We had a hundred
and forty batteries across the approaches and between them they had recorded, in sixty years, one
engagement that ended in a hit.

So the pamphlets wrote themselves. *Monuments to the men who ordered them.* One of the better ones
called the northern chain **a row of gravestones facing the wrong way**, and I remember laughing,
because it was a good line and because I had helped supply the figures behind it.

And the serious argument — the one I still cannot fault on its own terms — was this: the dark does not
come to you. If something is out there, it is out *there*, and a wall does not find it. Build hulls.
Go and look. Take it to them.

So we did. We built the Ninth and the Eleventh out of the northern chain's allocation, and I did the
costing personally, and it was clean work, and I was proud of it.

**They left in AU 69.** To patrol, and to survey, and to be somewhere useful instead of bolted to a
rock. I watched them go up. Everybody did. There were children on the roofs.

---

### What nobody costed

I am going to be exact, because exactness is the only thing I am still good for.

A ship that has left is **gone**. Not unreachable — we could talk to them, we talked to them every day,
the Whisper does not care about distance. Gone in the only way that matters: nothing can be *sent*
back. Voice arrives instantly and hulls arrive when they arrive, and between those two facts is the
whole of what happened to us.

We built things that could go anywhere, and in doing so we built things that could not be here.

---

### The eleventh of the month

They did not appear on an approach. Nobody sees anything coming; that is not a failure of our watch,
that is the shape of the universe. Above light speed a ship receives nothing and is seen by nothing,
and it arrives with its own news.

At 04:20 the sky over the Gate was ordinary. At 04:21 it was not there.

I have heard people say *they blocked out the stars* and I want to correct it, because the truth is
worse and I was outside. They did not block the stars. **They blocked the moons.** Both of them, and
the moons are large and close and everybody on that world has looked at them every night of their
lives, and at 04:21 there was simply hull where the moons went. Grey, and very slightly curved, and it
kept going past where a thing should stop.

Titan Lords. Nine of them, and I use the number because I counted, and counting is what I am.

Nobody screamed. I want that on the record too, because it surprised me. Ninety thousand people came
out of their houses and stood in the street and looked up, and the sound was like a held breath, and
somewhere behind me a woman said *oh* in the voice you use when you have dropped something.

---

### What the batteries did

They fired at 04:23.

Nobody ordered it. The order would have taken four minutes to compose and the batteries do not need
one — a battery has one job and it is standing on the thing it is for, and there is no journey and no
briefing and nothing to decide. That is the whole of what we called *no mobility* in AU 63. It means
**already there.** I costed that as a deficiency for eleven years.

The northern chain went first and it was not glorious. It was industrial. It sounded like a foundry
that had gone wrong, and it did not stop, and the light came up off the ground and went into the sky in
straight lines that did not bend, and I understood for the first time in my life what *fixed* means: a
turret does not miss because it moved. It is bolted to bedrock. It has spent sixty years learning
precisely one piece of sky.

The Titans could not answer that. They are enormous and they are slow and they were **in orbit**, which
is the one place a large thing cannot be subtle, and they had come expecting a world with a fleet in it
and found a world with a hundred and forty things that could not run away.

Battery Fourteen burned out its own mounting and kept firing off the deformation. Battery Nine
sheared and dropped forty metres onto the ridge below and the crew went down with it and the barrel was
still hot when we reached it two days later. Twenty-two of the hundred and forty were still able to
fire when it ended. Twenty-two.

The Ninth and the Eleventh heard all of it. Live, clear, instant, every word of it, from four hundred
and ten days away. Commodore Teyl asked for a situation report every hour for six hours and then
stopped asking, and the last thing she sent was not an order.

I have read that transcript. There is nothing in it I can use.

---

### The corridor

Four hulls came down. Three of them killed everything inside them on the way. The fourth came down
across the eastern quarries, mostly intact, and it had people in it.

You have to understand that no living person had ever done what we did next.

We have argued with eleven other species for seventy-four years. Daily. Bitterly. In detail. The
Whisper carries voice and nothing else, so every empire in this galaxy knows exactly what every other
empire *sounds like* and not one of us has stood in a room with them. I had negotiated with Titan
factors for nine years. I knew a Titan voice better than I know my brother's.

I had never been in a corridor with one.

It was dark and it was tilted about fifteen degrees and everything was too big — the treads too deep,
the handholds at the wrong height, the whole of it built for something with a longer stride. We had
cutting tools and shoring bars because that is what you bring into a wreck. That is what we used. I am
not going to be delicate about it: we used quarry tools on people in a corridor, and it took a long
time, and they were slow, and being slow is what the Titan Lords have chosen instead of fear.

The one I met was pinned at the hip under a spar and could not turn round, and it heard me coming for
a long while before I got there, and it did not struggle. It waited. When I came into its light it
looked at me for a while and it said — in perfectly good Registry standard, in that voice I had known
for nine years —

*"You are very small."*

Not afraid. Not hostile. **Surprised**, the way you are surprised by a fact you had been told and never
pictured. Seventy-four years of talking and neither of us had ever pictured the other.

I asked it why they had come. I had to. I was the ranking officer in that corridor and I had a hundred
and eighteen dead behind me and it was the only question.

It did not refuse. It genuinely tried. It said the sailing had been decided a long time ago and the
date had been on the order and the order had a date on it, and when I said *who decided*, it said a
name I did not know, and when I said *when*, it thought about it and said **before my grandmother's
sail.**

They had not come to take our world. They had come because it was on a list, and the list was older
than anyone aboard, and nobody in nine ships had thought to ask whether the reason still existed —
because in an empire that measures in ages, *the reason no longer exists* is not a sentence anybody has
a use for.

Ninety thousand people stood in a street and looked at the underside of an argument nobody alive had
had.

---

### What I have done since

I maintain the northern chain. That is my posting and I asked for it and I will not be moved from it,
which is a joke I permit myself about once a year.

I have not repudiated my AU 63 assessment. People want me to and I will not, because it was honest work
from the figures we had, and pretending I was secretly right all along would be a worse lie than the
one I actually told. What I did was worse than a mistake and smaller than a crime: **I costed a promise
as an asset and found it poor value.**

Deshan is alive and still has his eleven and has still never fired a shot that hit anything. I went to
him. I am not proud of why — I wanted him to take it from me, and I had a sentence ready about a hundred
and forty things that could not go anywhere standing exactly where they were and not stopping.

He let me finish it. Then he said, *"Yes,"* and asked whether Battery Nine's crew had been recovered,
and when I said they had he said good, and offered me tea, and that was the whole of it.

He did not want the apology and he does not need the vindication, because the thing he has always known
is not that turrets win battles. It is smaller and harder than that.

**A turret does not stay because it is brave. It stays because staying is the only thing it can do.**
Everything with a choice went where it was sent. The batteries were left behind for the same reason
they were still there at 04:23, and the reason is that they had nowhere else to be, and on precisely
one morning in sixty years that stopped being their weakness.

They could not be moved.

That is not a virtue. I have signed enough assessments to know the difference between a virtue and a
property. It is a property, and it is the only one that was any use to us, and I did not think it was
worth eighty metal.

## Notes

**The mechanic underneath is Law 1, twice.** Blind FTL is why nine capital hulls can be over a world at
04:21 having been absent at 04:20, and it is also why *take the battle to them* was never as sound as
it sounded — you cannot intercept what cannot be seen approaching. The pundits were not stupid. They
were arguing for a strategy the physics does not support, which is a much more realistic kind of wrong.

**And Law 11 is the tragedy.** The Whisper carries voice instantly and cargo not at all
(`../11-laws-of-the-world.md`). So the fleet heard its own world attacked in real time from four
hundred days away and could do nothing, and Commodore Teyl's last transmission is deliberately not
quoted. This is the most useful thing that law has done for the fiction so far and it costs nothing to
use again.

**Why the first face-to-face contact happens in a wreck.** Twelve civilisations have argued daily for
seventy-four years and never met, because voice travels and bodies do not. That makes the eastern
quarries the first physical meeting in living memory, and it happens in the dark, at an angle, with
quarry tools. *"You are very small"* is the line to protect: no threat, no defiance, just a fact
neither species had ever pictured.

**Weakness and strength, inverted twice.** The Titan Lords' doctrine is *be too large for it to
matter*, and largeness is what makes them unmissable in orbit and helpless in a corridor. Sesse's
precision is what made her assessment persuasive and what makes her account unbearable — she is still
counting, and she counts the batteries and the dead and the metal, because it is the only thing she has
left to offer.

**Pair with `../28-through-lines.md` T7.** Every other bet in the folder is a wager on knowledge you
cannot verify — Wren's deceleration, Halloway's certified corridor, Sten's recited lane, four
Mechanicus hulls at 9-D. A turret is the only purchase that is not a wager, for the same reason a swept
shoal is safe forever (Law 6): **it is standing on the ground it is a fact about.** Arran costed the one
line item in the game that cannot be wrong, and marked it poor value, and the game agrees with her
right up until it does not.

**Pair with T2, lightly and without stating it.** An order older than everyone executing it, whose
reason nobody thought to re-examine, is also what is walking towards 9-D — *requires heavier hull,
revisit*, annotated in AU 12, coming due this decade. Two empires, two orders that outlived their
authors. Nobody in either story knows about the other and no document should connect them.

**What must not be added.** No revenge. The Ninth and the Eleventh do not come home to a reckoning and
Teyl is not given a redemption; a fleet that could not return is the point, and giving it something to
do afterwards would spend the law for nothing. And Arran never says she was right in the end, because
she was not.

**Art beat.** Not the ships. The moons — two of them, familiar, and hull where they were. And later, at
ground level: a forty-metre gouge down a ridge with a barrel at the bottom of it, still too hot to
touch.

**VO note.** Flat, procedural, and faster than the material wants, exactly like Halloway. She has told
this before and she has a version. The only two places to let anything through are *oh* and *you are
very small*, and both should be delivered as quotations of somebody else, because they are.
