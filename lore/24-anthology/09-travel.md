# Travel

Six pieces, six of six done. Covers the Propulsion branch (Ion Drives, Warp Drives), traces, the Whisper,
and running dark. Per Law 1 the blindness is absolute — nothing here softens it.

---

## Running Dark — "The Interval"

Every crossing has an interval and every crew handles it differently.

Between departure and arrival there is no information. Not poor information — none. The hull is above light
speed and anything ahead of it arrives simultaneously with the hull, so for however many hours or days the
crossing takes, the ship is a sealed room travelling toward an unread answer.

Rating Deshan has crossed two hundred and forty times.

"Some ships work through it. Ours plays cards. There was a destroyer at Ordel that held a *choir*, badly,
which I thought was mad until I did a crossing with them."

The one thing nobody does is watch the plot, because there is nothing on it.

"New ratings watch the plot. You let them do it once. About four hours in they understand that the display
is not going to change and they go and find the cards."

He has been asked what he thinks about during the interval.

"Nothing useful. I have tried to make it into something. It is not something. It is four days in a room,
and then either the room arrives somewhere or it does not, and the arriving takes about a second and is
the whole of it."

## Ion Drives · Lv3 — "Eight Per Cent Each"

The Propulsion branch does not make you faster. It makes movement *cheaper*, eight per cent a level, and
the difference between that and speed is the difference between an empire that can manoeuvre and one that
cannot.

Quartermaster Tanu runs the reckoning ledger at Ordel Gate and she is the only person who has ever been
pleased about Ion Drives.

"Fleet commanders want speed. Speed is not on the tree. What is on the tree is *not paying so much*, and
every eight per cent I claw back is a probe I can send instead."

She keeps the arithmetic on a board in her office. At level nought, moving the fleet from Ordel to Vail
cost four hundred and eleven of reckoning. At level three it costs three hundred and eleven.

"A hundred back. That is one probe and change. One probe is one sector's truth. So Ion Drives level three
is worth precisely *one fact per fleet movement*, forever, and nobody has ever thanked the propulsion
branch for a fact."

She has the board photographed annually for the file. Nobody has ever asked for it.

## Warp Drives · Lv3 — "The Cap"

The move-cost discount caps at sixty per cent and Chief Constructor Vail can tell you why in one sentence:
below that, the reckoning stops being the cost and the *ship* becomes the cost.

"You cannot make a crossing free. At some point what you are spending is hull fatigue and crew, and no
research reduces those."

She has built the drives for four fleets and describes level three as "the last honest improvement."

"Past it the returns are in the noise and the mass penalty is not. There is a fourth level in the Concord
schedules. We can read it. We are not going to build it."

Asked why not, given that the Concord clearly did.

"Because the Concord had lit lanes. On a lit lane you know what is ahead and you can run the drive at the
edge of tolerance. Running at the edge of tolerance while blind is how you arrive at a shoal with no
margin."

Her note in the technical file is one line and has been quoted in three navies' training material.

**"The Concord's fourth level assumes a Lamp. Everything they built assumes a Lamp. That is the whole
difficulty with inheriting their library."**

## A Trace — "What You Are Actually Buying"

Keth of the Fleet sells routes and has developed a demonstration for people who think they are buying a
line on a map.

He puts two charts on the table. Same sectors, same line, same endpoints.

"That one was flown in the spring. That one was flown in AU 51 by a man who is dead. Identical drawing.
Completely different object."

Then he explains what a trace actually contains, which is not geometry.

"It is a *survival*. Somebody went that way and came back and wrote down what the crossing cost. If the
answer was *nothing*, that is a clean trace and it is the most valuable paper in this galaxy. If the answer
was *four hulls*, that is still worth having, because you now know to send four you can spare."

And then the part that costs him sales.

"Gravel drifts. A trace is a fact about one crossing on one day. Twenty years on it is a rumour with
paperwork, and I will tell you that before you pay me, and I have lost accounts over it, and I will keep
doing it."

## A Warp Gate — "Two Sectors"

Chief Constructor Vail's third gate spans two sectors and she can describe exactly what it is like to use,
which almost nobody can, because the crews who use it do not talk about it.

"You go in and you are not blind. That is all. For the length of the span — two sectors, about forty
minutes — the ship knows what is in front of it."

Rating Deshan has crossed a gate eleven times and describes it as the most upsetting duty he has ever
had.

"Everything is fine. The plot *updates*. It shows you what is coming and it is right, and it keeps being
right, for forty minutes."

"And then you come out the far end and it stops, and you are in the dark again, and you have just been
reminded what it is supposed to be like."

He has requested not to be posted to gate transit again. The request was granted without comment, which
suggests he is not the first.

"My grandmother did nine hundred years of that. Her whole life. Everyone's whole life. And we get forty
minutes of it, at a cost that would fund a navy, and we call it an achievement."

## The Whisper — "You Can Hear Everything and Go Nowhere"

The relays survived. Nobody knows why.

Signals Rating Ombra works the Whisper desk at Ordel Gate and the traffic she carries is the cruellest
thing in the galaxy.

"Voice and text, instantly, anywhere. Twelve empires, all of them reachable. I can put a Nomad broker
through to a Titan house in about four seconds."

What it cannot carry is anything with mass.

"So there is a family on my board. Been on it since before I had the desk. Mother on Bell, son on a Nomad
convoy that was two sectors out when the Lamps went, ended up on the wrong side of the Deep. They talk
every week. Seventy-four years, mother to son and now son to grandson, and no living member of that
family has ever been in a room with another one."

She has read the traffic. It is not tragic to listen to. It is domestic — weather, work, a bad knee.

"That is what breaks you about this job. It is not grief. It is *ordinary*, for seventy-four years, across
a distance nobody can cross."

She has never told them she has read it. There is no procedure that would let her.
