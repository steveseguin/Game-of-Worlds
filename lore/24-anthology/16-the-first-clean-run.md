# 16 · The First Clean Run

The only piece in the anthology written in the licensed elevated register (`../03-themes.md`, "The one
licensed elevation"). It exists because Law 6 tells us to treat swept ground as sacred and a hundred and
five pieces had been treating it as an expense.

Present tense, first person, in the moment. Nothing else in this folder is.

---

## Courier Tolt, Bell to Kettering, the eleventh of Third — filed on arrival

Eleven sectors.

I want to write that down before I do anything else, before the debrief and the sleep and the noise,
because in about ninety minutes somebody is going to make this administrative and I would like there to
be one copy that is not.

Eleven sectors, end to end, Bell to Kettering, and every one of them swept.

---

I have flown for nineteen years. I want to explain what a crossing is, for anybody who has not done it,
because everybody thinks it is speed and it is not. It is **arithmetic.**

You take the trace. You look at how old it is, and who flew it, and whether they were good, and whether
anything has drifted since. You cost it. You decide what you will accept — I will take one shoal at
transit odds, I will not take two, I will not take one unswept at arrival unless the cargo is a person.
And then you go, and for the length of the crossing you cannot see anything at all, and you do the sum
again in your head about every four seconds because there is nothing else to do with your hands.

Nineteen years. Two thousand, one hundred and something crossings. Every single one of them, that sum.

I did not do it today.

---

The chain went in over ninety-one years and nobody built it. That is the part I keep coming back to. It
was not a project. There was no plan and no office and nobody's name on it. Four empires swept eleven
rocks for eleven separate reasons across three generations, none of them talking to the others, most of
them not liking each other, and the sectors happened to be in a line.

Ordel's Bar went in AU 6, which is so early that the people who did it were still calling it *clearing.*
Anselm's Reach was Terran and cost them nine hulls and there is a stamped plate on it. Two of the middle
five are Nomad and they will not say what they paid. The Vail Shoal was swept by somebody whose empire
does not exist any more.

And the ninth — the ninth was the gap. Two hundred years of drift right in the middle, and it broke the
chain into two useless halves, and it sat there my whole career. Everybody knew about the ninth. It was
a joke, the way you make jokes about weather.

They finished it last Fourth. Sixteen hulls, over four seasons, and I will be honest and say I did not
follow it closely because things like that do not finish.

---

I lifted from Bell at 06:00 with a schedule that assumed the old routing.

The first three are ordinary. I have flown them a hundred times each and they are swept and I know they
are swept, and I still did the sum, because nineteen years.

The second is Anselm's Reach.

I should say something about that and I am going to keep it short. Everybody at Bell knows the Reach.
Forty minutes, and it was the longest single lane in the galaxy when there were lanes, and Wren flew it
two hundred and six times and was inside it on the day, and decelerated, which is the one thing we are
taught never to do, and came out sideways and alive. They still teach the crossing. They do not teach
what she saw, because she will not say.

I went through the Reach this morning at rated speed without thinking about it.

I want to be accurate: I did think about it, but afterwards, at Kettering, sitting down. At the time it
was the second waypoint and it was swept and I did not do the sum. The most dangerous forty minutes
anybody of my profession has ever flown is now the part of my route where nothing happens.

It was somewhere in the fourth that I stopped.

Not decided to. **Ran out of things to calculate.** I reached for the next number and there was not one
— no drift risk, no age on the trace, nothing to weigh, nothing to accept. It is charted. It is cleared.
It is corridored. Somebody is standing on it. It cannot hurt me and it cannot hurt the next one either.

And I had about eleven minutes of that.

I do not know how to write down what that was like and I am not going to reach for a word, so I will
just put the fact: **for eleven minutes I was the fastest thing in the galaxy and nothing in front of me
could kill me, and that has not been true for anybody since the day the Lamps went out.**

I could not see. Nobody can see; that is not what a swept chain gives you. It is better than seeing. It
is not *needing* to.

---

I did the last four at full rated speed. Full. I have never done that in my life, not once, not on the
best trace I ever bought. I came into the Kettering approaches so far ahead of my own schedule that the
berth was not ready and Onn came out personally to ask whether I had lost cargo.

Bell to Kettering. Nineteen years of my life, and the answer is **four hours and nine minutes.**

Onn wrote it up. He says it is the longest continuous crossing anybody has made since AU 0 and he wanted
to know how it felt and I said *fine* and went and sat down for a while behind the reckoning shed, which
he had the manners not to mention.

---

Here is what I actually want on the record, and then I will go and be debriefed.

It was not mine. I did not do anything. I was carried the whole way by a hundred and forty years of
people who are dead, who did not know each other, who were not building this, and who each bought one
rock for their own reasons and made it safe **forever** — and forever turned out to mean that today a
courier who was not born yet could run their eleven sectors end to end at full speed and never once do
the sum.

Nine hulls at Anselm's Reach. Sixteen at the ninth. Whatever the Nomads will not tell me. Somebody's
whole crew, on some rock in the middle, in a year I would have to look up.

And the thing that is making it hard to sit still is not the record and it is not the eleven minutes.

**It is that it can be done again tomorrow by somebody who is not good.**

That is what they bought. Not a feat — a *road.* Any fool can drive down it. I have spent nineteen years
being very good at a thing nobody should have had to be good at, and this morning, for eleven minutes,
being good was not required, and I would give the entire record back to have that be true everywhere.

It will be. One rock at a time, apparently. Ninety-one years and nobody in charge.

Four hours nine. Filed.

— *Tolt*

## Notes

**Why this piece exists, stated plainly.** The report card graded Emotional range **C+** for a hundred
and sixty thousand words, and the cause was not a shortage of material — it was
`../03-themes.md`'s locked register, which is superb at cost and forbids awe. Nobody in this folder was
permitted to be *proud*. Meanwhile Law 6 instructs us to treat swept ground as **sacred**. The charter
now licenses elevation for that one subject and this is the model piece.

**Nothing here breaks Law 1, and the restraint is the point.** A swept corridor is not lit. Tolt cannot
see and says so twice. What she gets is the removal of the arithmetic, which is a truer and stranger
gift than sight, and it is exactly what `SECTOR_TYPES` actually gives a player: `hazardous` gone,
permanently, because somebody holds it.

**The pride is inheritance, not achievement**, which is rule 1 of the licence and the whole reason the
piece is not the register we mock in `../03-themes.md`. Tolt sets a record and does not care about it.
She is a beneficiary of ninety-one years of strangers, most of whom disliked each other, none of whom
were building a road. That is Theme 1 — *knowledge is bought and somebody else always paid for it* —
finally arriving as a **gift** rather than as a debt.

**The last line is the thesis of the game.** *It can be done again tomorrow by somebody who is not
good.* A road does not need heroes, and that is what makes Law 6 the only positive-sum thing in the
setting. Do not let a later draft give Tolt a triumph; her one moment of feeling is sitting down behind
a shed, and Onn has the manners not to mention it.

**Anselm's Reach makes T7's two poles the same piece of sky, and this was not planned.** Wren's testimony
(`../10-the-long-file/05-void-walkers.md`) is *"forty minutes, Bell to Anselm's Reach, longest lane in the
galaxy,"* flown two hundred and six times, and she was inside it on the day and survived by decelerating.
It is the folder's one bet that paid. Seventy-four years later it is **waypoint two** of a route where
nothing happens, crossed at rated speed by a courier who did not think about it until afterwards.

The same ground, at the two ends of the setting's argument: the most dangerous forty minutes anyone ever
flew, and a stretch of road. Nothing had to be invented to connect them — Wren's lane simply *is* on the
chain, and noticing that is what the through-line file is for.

**On "longest lane" versus four hours nine.** Not a contradiction, and the distinction is load-bearing. A
**lane** is one Concord hop and the Reach was the longest of them. A **chain** is eleven swept sectors
strung end to end, which is a *route* and is not lane infrastructure at all. The old galaxy had long
lanes; this one is growing long roads, badly, out of unrelated purchases. That difference is the whole
of what changed.

**Pair with `../28-through-lines.md` T7 and T8.** T7 is the bet, and every other entry on it is a price.
This is the one row where the ledger comes out ahead, and it is the same fact as T8's unspoken answer:
knowledge about ground you hold cannot go stale, so a swept sector is the only permanent thing anybody
owns. Halloway's corridor decayed. Sten's recited lane decayed. **The eleven did not, and cannot.**

**The ninth is deliberately mundane.** Two hundred years of drift, a joke people made about the weather,
finished by sixteen hulls over four seasons while Tolt was not paying attention. Epic events in this
setting are assembled out of unglamorous procurement, and a reader who has swept a shoal in-game has
done precisely this and should recognise it.

**What must not be added.** No enemy. No obstacle. Nothing goes wrong. This is the only piece in the
folder where nothing goes wrong and that is its function; the tension of the entire anthology is what
makes four hours nine minutes land. And no second elevated piece without a real reason — the ratio is
one in a hundred and six, and it is doing the work.

**Art beat.** Not the ship. The stamped plate at Anselm's Reach, nine hulls' worth, two hundred years
weathered and still legible — and then the same shot from a courier's approach at full rated speed,
where it is a streak you would miss if you did not know it was there.

**VO note.** She is writing, not speaking, and she is in a hurry to get it down before somebody makes it
administrative. Brisk, plain, faster than the material. The two places to let anything through are *I do
not know how to write down what that was like* and *four hours nine* — and the second one should be
flat, because she has already used up everything she had.
